\begin{abstract}
CLIP assigns text-based scores to every ordinal grade, from age groups to image-quality levels, yet grades without labeled adaptation images can disappear from its predictions. We show that an unseen grade poses two distinct problems: how often it is predicted, and how its images are ordered against neighboring grades. Class-bias calibration restores predictions but provably cannot change fixed-pair image ranking. Blending toward zero-shot changes ranking only along a single path. Writing adapted scores as a zero-shot reference plus an image-dependent displacement explains the gap: a missing grade's displacement was never directly fitted using images of that grade, while neighboring grades learned from related visual evidence. We introduce TIE, a training-free method that transfers neighbors' adaptation displacements while preserving each missing grade's zero-shot reference. TIE leaves seen-grade scores unchanged and exactly restores the zero-shot margin between a missing endpoint and its nearest seen grade. Across four benchmarks, nine adapter configurations, and six missing-grade patterns, TIE with matched calibration lowers missing-grade and overall error on every benchmark and improves missing--seen ranking in 34 of 36 adapter--dataset settings. In a controlled interior-grade comparison, TIE yields positive paired 95\% intervals at 59 of 64 matched dataset--strength comparisons. On three benchmarks, TIE at the original strength beats the best of 17 shared blending strengths in the adapter-averaged comparison, even when Blend is selected on test results. Ordinal neighbors thus provide what calibration and global blending cannot: a source of task adaptation for grades that training never saw.
\end{abstract}
