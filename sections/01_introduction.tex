\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\input{fig/fig1_page2}

Many visual recognition tasks are ordinal: a face belongs to an age group, a photograph to a decade, an image to a quality level.
Vision--language models such as CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} suit these tasks well, because every grade can be named in text, and prompt learning adapts them to a task with limited labeled data~\cite{zhou2022coop,zhou2022cocoop,li2022ordinalclip,wang2023l2rclip}.
But limited labels do not always cover every grade.
We study grades that are \emph{missing} from the adaptation set: they are unseen during adaptation, yet the deployed model must still predict them.
CLIP keeps a text classifier for each missing grade, but adaptation can suppress their predictions~\cite{mai2024fine}: in Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}, the adapted model does not recognize a single test image of the missing grade \emph{fair}.

The standard diagnosis is miscalibration: adaptation inflates the scores of seen grades, and a class-wise offset should bring the missing ones back~\cite{chao2016gzsl,mai2024fine}.
It does bring back predictions---calibration lifts the recall of \emph{fair} from 0\% to 22\%.
It cannot bring back the order.
Rank the test images of \emph{fair} and of its seen neighbor \emph{poor} by the margin between their two class scores.
Any class-wise offset, even combined with a shared temperature, leaves this ranking exactly unchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:cal}).
The pair AUROC stays at 0.526, well below the 0.617 of the zero-shot model.
Calibration changes how often a missing grade is predicted, not how its images are ordered.
Fixing the score is not fixing the order.

To see what is missing, we write the adapted score as a zero-shot reference plus an adaptation displacement, $S=T+D$.
A missing grade keeps its reference $T_m$, but its displacement $D_m$ receives no direct supervision from images of that grade.
Calibration only \emph{shifts} the missing grade's score by a constant.
A related score-space alternative, motivated by model interpolation~\cite{wortsman2022robust}, \emph{scales} the displacements of all grades at once: $T+\lambda D$.
This does change the order, but only along a single path through the zero-shot ($\lambda{=}0$) and adapted ($\lambda{=}1$) models, and calibrating the blend adds no new orders.
Neither operation supplies the missing grade with the grade-specific adaptation it lacks.
What matters is not merely how much adaptation is kept, but where the missing grade's displacement comes from.

Ordinal label spaces offer a source that nominal ones do not.
Nearby grades share task-relevant visual characteristics, and their displacements are adapted using labeled images from those seen grades.
We propose \textbf{TIE}, which transfers this adaptation along the grade order: for every image, a missing grade inherits the displacement of its nearest seen grades while keeping its own zero-shot reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:teaser}b).
TIE needs no additional training and operates on the scores of different CLIP adapters.
It leaves every seen-grade score untouched, and at an endpoint grade it provably recovers the zero-shot order with its nearest seen neighbor.
Calibration then sets the operating point: TIE repairs the order, and calibration fixes the score.

We evaluate TIE on four ordinal benchmarks covering age, historical decade, and image quality, with nine adaptation configurations and six missing-grade patterns.
With the same calibration protocol applied to both, TIE lowers the missing-grade and overall mean absolute error of the adapted model on all four benchmarks and improves missing--seen ranking (MS-AUC) for 34 of 36 adapter--dataset settings.
Because TIE commutes with blending, we can also isolate its effect: at matched strength, the two paths share every seen-grade score and differ only in where the missing grade's displacement comes from (Fig.~\ref{fig:matched_strength}).
Paired confidence intervals support a gain at 59 of 64 evaluated nonzero dataset--strength combinations.
On three benchmarks, none of the 17 shared blending strengths---not even the best one, chosen on test performance---reaches TIE at the original strength in the adapter-averaged comparison.
There, TIE also surpasses the zero-shot model, so its gain is transfer, not a return to zero-shot.
The gain is largest when a grade has no labeled training images.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Finding.} For grades missing from adaptation, calibration and ranking separate: class-wise calibration cannot reorder missing-grade images against their neighbors, and blending toward zero-shot moves this order only along a single path.
  \item \textbf{Method.} We decompose adapted scores into a zero-shot reference and an adaptation displacement, and propose TIE, a training-free ordinal displacement transfer that preserves seen-grade scores and exactly recovers the zero-shot order at endpoint grades.
  \item \textbf{Evidence.} Across four benchmarks, nine adaptation configurations, and six missing-grade patterns, TIE improves calibrated classification error and missing--seen ranking, outperforms blending in a controlled matched-strength comparison, and helps most when a grade has no supervision.
\end{itemize}
